# The professional standard — a specialist presenting to specialists

Read at Stage 3 (narrative), apply at Stage 4 (skeleton), check at Stage 6 (quality gate).

The test for every slide is one question: **would a congress programme committee in this
specialty accept it as it stands?** Not "is it clear", not "is it attractive" — would a panel
of senior specialists, comparing it with the best talks at their own meeting, pass it without
a note. A slide that is punchy but informal fails. A slide that is correct but reads like a
student handout fails.

These rules were learned when a deck that had passed every density and design check was
still judged "שכונתי" — informal, neighbourhood-grade — by a specialist. Every rule below
names what that deck did.

---

## 1. Headlines state the finding, in the register of a paper

- **Every content slide's title is a complete, factual assertion** — the claim the slide
  proves (the assertion–evidence model): *"Documented HF diagnosis was associated with 470 mL
  less intraoperative fluid"*, not *"Why the diagnosis matters"*.
- **No slogans, wordplay, antithesis or rhetorical punchlines on screen.** "The water didn't
  accumulate. It was pushed." / "The report is right. The conclusion is missing." / "Four
  orders, not assumptions" were all rejected. The same idea in a paper's register — *"Flash
  pulmonary edema: volume redistribution"* — is accepted. Rhetoric belongs in the spoken
  notes, where the presenter's voice carries it.
- **Ink beats are factual statements too.** "LVEF תקין אינו שולל אי־ספיקת לב" is a beat a
  committee accepts; "EF תקין. לב לא תקין." reads as a tagline.
- **No quoted hallway speech on screen** ("She's dry, let's give fluids"). Describe the
  behaviour: *"Intuitive response: fluid bolus"*.
- Titles are one line. At 40pt that is roughly 36–40 Hebrew characters, fewer when English
  terms are mixed in. A wrapped title collides with the content under it — check the render.

## 2. Terminology in the register clinicians actually use

- In a non-English deck, **drug names, physiological parameters, device modes and trial names
  stay in English**, exactly as specialists write and say them: Norepinephrine, Preload,
  Afterload, Stroke volume, atrial kick, PEEP, B-lines, Trendelenburg. Invented or academy
  translations ("קדם־עומס", "עומס אחורי", "הכיווץ העלייתי") read as a translated textbook.
- The surrounding language is formal and precise: "מטופלת", "התוויה", "מומלץ", "בדרגת
  המלצה", "תצפיתי". No colloquialisms, no second-person imperatives on screen.
- Abbreviations are the field's standard ones (HFpEF, GDMT, MRA, euDKA, POAF) and are
  spelled out once in the notes.

## 3. Visuals carry data or mechanism — never decoration

- **No generic icon sets** (a pill, a bed, a prohibition sign, a magnifying glass). To a
  specialist audience they are clip-art and signal a template. A tile is anchored by its
  content, a kicker ("Class I", "01"), or nothing.
- **No invented quantitative curves.** A chart implies data. A "fluid mobilisation peaks on day
  2–3" area chart drawn from nothing was rejected. If a curve is a teaching schematic (a
  pressure–volume relation), say so on screen — *"schematic; values illustrative"* — and keep
  the axes physiologically honest.
- A case monitor must be clinically real: AF looks irregularly irregular, vitals are
  internally consistent (MAP matches the pressure), totals add up across slides (fluid given
  only increases).

## 4. Every recommendation shows its authority

- A recommendation slide names **the body, the year, and the class/level** where one exists:
  *"ESC 2026 HF · Class I"*, *"ACC/AHA 2024 · 2a"*. Put it on the slide, in the readout strip.
- **Expert opinion is labelled as expert opinion** — *"המלצות מומחים · אין RCT ייעודי"*.
  Presenting consensus as evidence is the fastest way to lose an expert room.
- Observational data that contradicts a guideline goes on screen as **contested**, with its
  design and main limitation, and the slide says the guideline stands.

## 5. Guidelines current to the day of the talk

Definitions and classes change. In one September the ESC removed HFmrEF, redefined HFpEF as
LVEF ≥50%, and made SGLT2i + MRA foundational therapy — two weeks before the deck was reviewed.

- At Stage 2, **search the major societies of the field for anything published in the last 18
  months**, by name: *"2026 ESC guidelines heart failure"*, *"2024 ACC/AHA perioperative
  guideline"*. Do not rely on the guideline you remember.
- Know the release calendar and check it when the talk is near one: ESC (late August), AHA
  (November), ACC (March–April), ADA Standards (December–January), ASA (October), ERS
  (September), ESA (June).
- A deck that cites a superseded guideline as current fails the gate, however good the rest is.

## 6. Clinical sequences get a safety review

Any slide that orders actions — an algorithm, a crisis sequence, a drug plan — is reviewed
**step by step against the case's own numbers** before it ships:

- Is any step contraindicated at the vitals shown? (Nitroglycerin listed before the
  vasopressor, at a systolic pressure of 78, was a real defect.)
- Are the immediate, zero-cost actions first? (Stop the fluids and give 100% oxygen before
  anything else; "stop fluids" had been listed seventh.)
- Does the sequence match current resuscitation and specialty guidance (unstable tachyarrhythmia
  → synchronised cardioversion without delay)?
- Is the drug-by-drug advice consistent with the perioperative guideline for *this* patient
  group? (Holding an ACE inhibitor on the day of surgery is reasonable in hypertension; in
  compensated HF the 2024 ACC/AHA guideline favours continuing GDMT except SGLT2i.)

## 7. Case presentation

- Anonymise: *"אישה בת 74"* on screen, not a first name. A consistent pseudonym in the notes is
  acceptable if the presenter prefers it.
- Keep the case numbers consistent across every slide that mentions them.
- For Morph to animate the deterioration, **monitor frames must be adjacent slides**: a stable
  frame immediately before the change (pre-induction → post-induction; stable at 60 min →
  crisis at 90 min). A monitor after a text slide does not animate.

## 8. Congress formalities — ask, never invent

- Title slide: presenter name, specialty, institution.
- Disclosure slide straight after the title when the venue requires it (congresses almost
  always do). Ask for the content; never write "no conflicts" on the presenter's behalf.
- References slide in AMA style; backup slides after the closing slide for the detail cut
  from the timed talk.
- A closing slide that is "תודה · שאלות ודיון", with the case outcome in the notes — not a
  narrative punchline on screen.

## 9. Hebrew-specific professional conventions

- Quotation marks ״…״; no arrow glyphs (→ ←) in running text — they flip unpredictably in RTL;
  use a colon, a comma, or "·".
- Dates DD/MM/YYYY. No nikud in professional text.
- Numbers, units and English terms inside Hebrew are handled by the library's bidi repair; do
  not end a Hebrew line with an English parenthetical.
- Hebrew tables: the first column (drug, parameter) sits at the right.

## 10. Speaker notes in the same register

The notes are spoken, so they may be warmer than the slides — first person, one question to the
audience at a natural decision point — but they stay professional: no slang, no jokes at the
expense of colleagues' practice, every study's limitation stated once, every recommendation
attributed ("ההנחיה האמריקאית מ־2024 ממליצה…").
